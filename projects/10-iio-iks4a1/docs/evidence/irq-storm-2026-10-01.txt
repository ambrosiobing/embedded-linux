The interrupt storm that disabled IRQ 185
=========================================

Thursday 1 October 2026, first attempt at criterion 4.

    iio-rate fifo lsm6dsv16x_accel 480 1
    [42640.612867] Disabling IRQ #185
    --- interrupts 100001  (10000.10/s)
    --- samples/s  0

The kernel's spurious-interrupt watchdog disabled the line after 100001
interrupts, almost all of them unhandled. The second run then reported
zero interrupts, because there was no longer an interrupt to count.

WHAT IS NOT WRONG, established before guessing at what is.

    INT1_CTRL  0x0d = 0x08     bit 3, INT1_FIFO_TH
    INT2_CTRL  0x0e = 0x00
    PIN_CTRL   0x03 = 0x00
    handler    st_lsm6dsx_handler_thread
    gpio-24    in, lo, IRQ

The part IS driving INT1, on the FIFO threshold, which is what the driver
should set for a watermark. The overlay's st,drdy-int-pin = 1 took effect.
Nothing is routed to INT2. The kernel's handler is the driver's own.

And the wire reaches it. A disconnected or undriven pin floats and storms
whenever anything disturbs it; this line was quiet for eleven hours and
stormed only while the buffer was enabled, which is only when the part
drives it. So Wednesday's wiring to CN9 pin 6 is confirmed a third time,
now by the behaviour of the signal rather than by a table or a node.

THE ARITHMETIC IS THE WHOLE DIAGNOSIS. 10000 interrupts a second against
a 480 Hz output rate is about twenty edges for every sample the part
produces. The sensor cannot generate that. Something is multiplying real
edges into many.

The leading candidate is the wire. The interrupt line is a loose jumper of
the same length as the I2C pair, unterminated, with no series resistance,
running beside two lines that switch at 400 kHz. A slow or ringing edge on
such a wire crosses the input threshold several times, and an edge-
triggered input counts every crossing. The driver's handler services the
first, finds data, returns IRQ_HANDLED, and the rest find an empty FIFO and
return IRQ_NONE. That is exactly the ratio the kernel saw.

THE PROJECT ALREADY KNEW THIS AND ONLY WROTE IT DOWN FOR I2C. BRINGUP step
0 says jumpers must be short, because 400 kHz over 20 cm of loose wire is
the documented limit and the failure is intermittent rather than clean.
The same physics applies to an interrupt line and nobody said so. A data
line that rings gives a corrupted byte; an interrupt line that rings gives
a storm, and the storm is louder and easier to diagnose.

THE TEST, stated before it is run. At a watermark of 64 the part asserts
INT1 at 480/64 = 7.5 times a second instead of 480. If ringing is
multiplying each real edge by about twenty, that is 150 spurious
interrupts a second rather than 10000, which is far below the watchdog's
threshold and should simply work.

  watermark 64 runs clean      the edge RATE was the problem, so the
                               line rings and a shorter wire or a series
                               resistor is the fix
  watermark 64 storms too      the multiplication is not rate-dependent
                               and the cause is elsewhere

Either way criterion 4's interesting case is the one that can be measured
first, which is a convenient accident rather than a plan.

THE RESULT, and the prediction was right by a hair
===================================================

    iio-rate fifo lsm6dsv16x_accel 480 64

    samples/s   467        4672 in 10 s against 4800 expected, 97.3 %
    interrupts  171.5/s    1715 in 10 s
    reader cpu  0.80 %
    ts sd       0.124 us, driver-interpolated, not a latency measurement

IT RAN. The watermark 64 case does not storm, which was the prediction.

AND THE RINGING IS NOW MEASURED RATHER THAN SUPPOSED. At 480 Hz with a
watermark of 64 the FIFO threshold fires 7.5 times a second. The kernel
counted 171.5.

    171.5 / 7.5 = 22.9 interrupts for every real assertion

The prediction written before the run was "about twenty".

THE SAME FACTOR APPEARS AT AN EDGE RATE SIXTY-FOUR TIMES HIGHER. The
watermark 1 run produced 10000 interrupts a second against a real 480.

    10000 / 480 = 20.8

20.8 and 22.9, from two runs whose real edge rates differ by a factor of
64. A multiplier that holds across that range is a property of the wire
and not of the sensor, the driver or the load. That is the ringing
hypothesis confirmed by something other than its own plausibility.

AND THE COMPARISON THE CRITERION EXISTS TO MAKE SURVIVES INTACT, because
the multiplication is common to both measurements and cancels in the
ratio:

    measured   10000 / 171.5 = 58.3
    theory     watermark 64 against watermark 1 = 64
    agreement  91 percent

So batching by 64 reduces the interrupt rate by about 58 times on this
bench. The number that is contaminated is the absolute rate; the ratio,
which is the actual claim, is not.

CRITERION 4 AS WRITTEN, honestly assessed.

  "fewer than 8 interrupts per second at watermark 64"
      measured 171.5, so it FAILS as written.
      The FIFO asserts 7.5 times a second, under the limit. The other
      164 are the wire. The criterion measures the bench's wiring and
      not the sensor's batching, in the same way criterion 8 measured
      the fixture and not the filter.

  "more than 400 per second at watermark 1"
      the real rate is 480 and the observed 10000, so it passes on
      either number, for the wrong reason in one case.

  "under 3 percent reader CPU"
      0.80 percent, PASSES, and this one is unaffected by the ringing
      because the spurious interrupts are serviced by the primary
      handler and never reach the reader.

WHAT WOULD MAKE THE ABSOLUTE NUMBER HONEST. A shorter interrupt jumper, a
series resistor of a few hundred ohms at the shield end, or both. The
project's own BRINGUP already says this about the I2C pair and never said
it about the interrupt line.

AND WATERMARK 1 CANNOT BE RUN ON THIS WIRING AT 480 Hz. 480 real
assertions a second times 23 is 11000 interrupts a second, which trips the
spurious watchdog in under ten seconds and disables the line. That is a
hard limit of the bench rather than of the part, and the comparison can
still be made at a lower rate: at 120 Hz, watermark 1 gives 120 x 23 =
2760 a second, which the watchdog tolerates.

THE 120 Hz PAIR, AND A WITHDRAWAL
==================================

    rate  wm   real int/s   measured   multiplier  samples/s   cpu
     480   1       480.00    10000.0        20.8          -   0.70%
     480  64         7.50      171.5        22.9        467   0.80%
     120   1       120.00     3816.8        31.8        116   0.40%
     120  64         1.88       95.9        51.1        115   0.20%

THE FIRST ROW IS NOT A MEASUREMENT AND I USED IT AS ONE. 100001 is the
kernel's spurious-interrupt threshold. The count did not reach 100001
because that is how many arrived in ten seconds; it stopped there because
the watchdog disabled the line. The run was aborted, not completed, and
10000 a second is a floor of unknown tightness.

SO THE CLAIM THAT THE MULTIPLIER IS CONSTANT IS WITHDRAWN. It rested on
20.8 and 22.9 agreeing across a sixty-fourfold change of edge rate, and
the 20.8 was an artefact. The three usable points give 22.9, 31.8 and
51.1, which is not a constant and therefore not a fixed ringing factor.

The paragraph that claimed it said the agreement confirmed the hypothesis
"by something other than its own plausibility". It did not. It confirmed
it by a number that the kernel had stopped counting.

WHAT REMAINS TRUE. Every measured interrupt rate is far above the FIFO's
real assertion rate, by factors of twenty to fifty, so spurious edges
dominate in every configuration. That much is solid in all four rows and
does not depend on the multiplier being constant.

What is not established is the mechanism. A simple ringing model predicts
a fixed multiplier and the data refuses it. The multiplier grows as the
assertion rate falls, which is the opposite of what a per-edge effect
does, and suggests a component that is roughly independent of how often
the part asserts. Nothing here identifies it.

AND THE CLEAN RESULT IS THE ONE THAT WAS NOT CONTAMINATED.

Reader CPU is unaffected by spurious interrupts, because they are
dismissed by the primary handler and never wake the reader. It behaves
exactly as it should in both dimensions:

    against sample rate, at watermark 64
        480 Hz  0.80 %      four times the samples
        120 Hz  0.20 %      four times the CPU

    against batching, at 120 Hz and identical throughput
        watermark  1  0.40 %
        watermark 64  0.20 %      half the CPU for the same data

Throughput is consistent across all of it: 467, 116 and 115 samples a
second against 480 and 120 expected, which is 96 to 97 percent everywhere,
the shortfall being the samples still in the FIFO when the buffer closes.

SO CRITERION 4 SPLITS CLEANLY ALONG WHAT THE WIRING TOUCHES.

    reader CPU under 3 percent          MET, 0.80 and 0.20 percent, and
                                        the batching benefit measured at
                                        exactly 2x on identical throughput

    interrupt counts                    NOT MEASURABLE on this bench.
                                        Spurious edges outnumber real
                                        assertions by twenty to fifty
                                        times and the mechanism is not
                                        characterised.

The honest reading is that this project can measure what batching costs
the reader and cannot yet measure what it saves the interrupt controller.
The second needs the interrupt line fixed, not more runs.
