WHAT THIS DEMONSTRATES. This chip has no driver in this kernel. Its
device tree node binds to nothing. /sys/bus/iio does not exist. And it is
delivering correct three-axis acceleration through eight bytes of i2c-dev
traffic. That is the argument Project 11 is built on, shown rather than
asserted: not every sensor needs a kernel driver to be useful.

WHAT IT DOES NOT DEMONSTRATE. Sample rate. Three i2cget calls per sample,
each a separate process, with a 0.2 s sleep, gives roughly five samples a
second against a part configured for 120. There are no timestamps. Nothing
here touches the hardware FIFO, the watermark interrupt on GPIO24, or any
of the buffered paths this project exists to compare. Those need the
drivers this kernel does not carry.

THE MAGNITUDE IS THE ACCEPTANCE CRITERION. The vector magnitude of a
stationary accelerometer must be one g, and it came out at 1.009 g, an
error of nine parts in a thousand. That is inside a MEMS part's normal
sensitivity tolerance and it needs no reference instrument, which is why
gravity is the right acceptance test for a bench with no calibration rig.

IT ALSO VALIDATES THE SCALE ASSUMPTION. The plot is titled "assumed two g
full scale, 16384 counts per g", and that assumption is now checked rather
than assumed: a wrong full-scale setting would have produced a magnitude
of about half a g or about two g, not 1.009. The register map and the
full-scale default are confirmed together by one number.

Note that the mean of the per-sample magnitudes, 1.009 g, is slightly
above the magnitude of the mean vector, 1.007 g. That is expected: noise
and the two disturbances add to the magnitude in both directions, and
averaging magnitudes cannot cancel them the way averaging components can.

TWO EVENTS ARE VISIBLE. A single sharp knock at about 24.5 s, seen as Y
falling to -0.67 g and Z dipping to 0.55 g in the same sample, and a burst
of oscillation between about 28 and 31 s on X, with Z reaching 1.21 g.

WHY THE EXTREMES ARE NOT THE PEAKS. At five samples a second, a knock
lasting a few milliseconds is caught by at most one sample and usually by
none. The -0.67 g on Y is one sample of a transient, not its maximum, and
the true peak of a tap on a desk is several g. So the min and max columns
above describe this capture rather than the events in it.

That is the clearest statement yet of what this project exists to fix. The
part was configured for 120 samples a second and delivered five, unevenly,
with no timestamps. The hardware FIFO and the watermark interrupt on
GPIO24 exist to take every one of the 120 with a timestamp attached, and
they need the drivers this kernel does not carry.

THE ANOMALY IS NOT PHYSICS. One sample near index 1660 has Z at 0.013 g
and a magnitude of 0.018 g. That is free fall, and a board resting on a
desk is not in free fall.

The discriminator is the magnitude. A tap, a lift or any real motion adds
to one or more axes and leaves the magnitude near or above one g. Only two
things drive all three axes to zero together: genuine free fall, which
would last hundreds of samples at this rate rather than one, and a
corrupted read.

THE LIKELY MECHANISM, and it is testable. Block data update is off by
default on this family. With it off, the output registers may be refreshed
between the reading of a low byte and its high byte, so one sample can be
assembled from two different measurements. The result is a single wild
value surrounded by correct ones, which is exactly the shape here.

CTRL3 at 0x12 carries the block data update bit. Setting it makes the
output registers hold still for the duration of a read. If the artefact
disappears from a recapture, the diagnosis is proven; if it survives, the
cause is elsewhere and worth finding before any of these numbers are used.

A CAVEAT ON THE DEDUPLICATION. Dropping consecutive identical reads
assumes identical means stale. At rest, with quantised output and low
noise, two consecutive genuine samples can also be identical, so the
unique count is a lower bound on the sensor's real output and the read
rate estimated from it is approximate.

THE BLOCK DATA UPDATE HYPOTHESIS IS WRONG. Measured the same evening:

  # i2cget -y 1 0x6b 0x12
  0x44
  # i2cset -y 1 0x6b 0x12 0x44
  # i2cget -y 1 0x6b 0x12
  0x44

0x44 is bit 6 and bit 2, block data update and register auto-increment,
and it read 0x44 BEFORE the write. That is this part's reset value for
CTRL3, so the output registers were already held still for the duration of
every read in the capture above, and the write changed nothing.

So a sample cannot have been assembled from two measurements, and the
near-zero magnitude has a different cause.

WHAT IS LEFT, in order of how much evidence there is for it.

One, the capture transport rather than the sensor. The three-axis run
earlier the same evening contains a visible block of serial corruption,
which is direct proof that the path from this board to a file can damage
characters. A single flipped or dropped character inside a hex field
produces a wrong number that still parses, with nothing to mark it.

Two, an I2C frame damaged on the wire. i2c-dev carries no integrity check
of any kind, the shield is on loose jumpers, and a wrong byte is returned
as data rather than as an error.

Three, the parse or the deduplication.

THE DISCRIMINATOR IS STRUCTURAL, not statistical. Go back to the raw dump
and look at the offending line itself, the six hex bytes and not the number
they were turned into:

  well-formed, six hex bytes, really decoding near zero
      the damage happened at or before the bus read
  short, long, or carrying a character that is not hex
      the damage happened in the transport or the parse, and the sensor
      and the bus are both innocent

Which is why the next capture carries a line index and a field count, so
that a damaged line is visible as a damaged line instead of arriving as a
plausible measurement.

THE NOISE FLOOR IS THE PART'S OWN. 0.36 mg on X and Y, 0.47 mg on Z, which
is about six counts. The data sheet gives a noise density of the order of
70 ug per root hertz, and over a bandwidth of about 60 Hz that is roughly
0.5 mg, so the measured floor is the sensor and nothing on this bench is
adding to it. Worth confirming against the data sheet figure for the mode
actually configured rather than resting on the order of magnitude.

THE EARLIER CAPTURE'S NOISE WAS NOT NOISE. The burst capture recorded above
reports X and Y extremes near 0.1 g and was read as a noise floor of a few
tens of milli-g. Against 0.36 mg measured here that is a factor of some
hundreds. Those extremes were the bench being disturbed during the
capture. A quiet run is not a formality: without one, every later number
is compared against a floor that was never measured.

THE ANOMALY DID NOT REPRODUCE. No sample below 0.5 g in 8000, no malformed
line, no read error. At the one in 1905 that the earlier capture appeared
to show, the expectation over 8000 samples is about four, and the chance of
seeing none is about one and a half percent. So the near-zero sample did
not come from a process running at that rate. The one thing that changed is
the path: this pair was written to a file on the board and copied as a
file. That is not proof, and a fault seen once and not since is not closed,
but the evidence now points at the terminal path and away from the bus.

THE DUPLICATE FRACTION FELL, AND THAT IS NOT AN IMPROVEMENT. 1.4 percent
quiet and 0.4 percent shaver, against 34 percent before. The arithmetic
that turned 34 percent into a read rate only works when the reads are
faster than the sensor: below that rate, samples are skipped rather than
repeated and the duplicate fraction sits near zero whatever the rate is.
So a near-zero duplicate fraction means the loop is at or below 120 Hz and
says nothing further. This loop is slower than the tight one, because it
pays for a command substitution and a redirect on every sample. The read
rate has to be timed rather than inferred.

THE WAVEFORM IS NOT THE SHAVER'S WAVEFORM. At or below 120 reads a second
the highest frequency that can be represented is 60 Hz, and a mains-driven
shaver vibrates at around 100 Hz. Whatever is above 60 Hz and survives the
sensor's own filter arrives folded down to a frequency that was never
present. The oscillation packets in the plot are that artefact. The RMS
figures survive it and are a lower bound, because the part band-limits
before it samples and the energy above its bandwidth is removed rather than
folded. Reading a frequency off this plot would be reading an artefact.

WHAT SURVIVES THE SAMPLING LIMIT, AND IS WORTH HAVING.

  Detection. 0.47 mg quiet against 10.66 mg on Z under the shaver is a
  factor of 23, so vibration well below what a hand feels is separable from
  the floor. That is enough for a running-or-not verdict and not enough for
  a spectrum.

  Direction. Z responds two and a half times as much as X and Y. The board
  lies flat, Z is normal to it, and a PCB on header pins is far more
  compliant out of plane than in it. That is the mechanics of the mounting,
  not a property of the sensor.

  Calibration. |a| is 1.0068 g in both runs where the true value is
  1.0000 g, so the sensitivity is 0.68 percent high. That is one number,
  it is inside the part's tolerance, and it is what a calibration step
  would null out.

  Repeatability. The three means agree between two independent runs to
  better than half a milli-g. That is what proves the shaver added
  acceleration without tilting the board, and it is also a stability figure
  for the bias in its own right.

THE ODR IS 120 Hz AND IT DOES NOT SCALE THIS AXIS. CTRL1 at 0x10 reads
0x06, and bits 3 to 0 hold the accelerometer output rate, with 0110 being
120 Hz. But the record is indexed by POLL, not by sensor sample. The sample
clock of this file is the shell loop's rate, which is unmeasured, jittery,
and unlocked from the sensor's own oscillator. Two clocks, and the axis
belongs to the wrong one. Assuming the loop ran near 120 Hz puts the peak
at roughly 3.2 Hz, and that assumption is the weakest link in the whole
calculation.

ALIASING IS PRESENT AND MEASURED. The shaver record is raised above the
quiet one by 10.4, 10.5 and 8.1 times in the top half of the band, from
0.25 to 0.5 cycles per sample, and 27.7 percent of the X axis AC energy
and 22.2 percent of the Y sits up there with no structure in it. A
narrowband mechanical source cannot lift an entire half-band uniformly.
That is folded content, which is to say real vibration arriving at
frequencies it never had.

THE TONE ITSELF IS REAL. All three axes peak in the SAME bin, 0.0270, one
part in two thousand, where the quiet record's strongest bins are scattered
at 0.0285, 0.0415 and 0.0258 and reach only 0.04 to 0.05 mg. So the shaver
puts a single forcing frequency into the board, which is what a motor does,
and the amplitudes 4.8, 1.3 and 1.1 mg on Z, Y and X follow the compliance
already measured: the board is most responsive normal to its plane.

WHAT IT IS NOT, PROBABLY. Under the 120 Hz assumption the tone sits near
3.2 Hz, which is far below any mechanism in a foil shaver. The reading that
fits is a beat: a mechanical line somewhere near 117 to 123 Hz against the
120 Hz sampling of the sensor itself, leaving the difference. A one percent
change in motor speed shifts the line by 1.2 Hz and the beat by the same,
which is exactly the wandering to 0.024 and 0.028 to 0.032 already noticed.

AND THAT IS A ONE COMMAND TEST, stated before it is run. Change the output
rate to 240 Hz with CTRL1 0x07, leave the loop alone, and recapture.

  the tone stays at 0.0270      it is mechanical, near 3.2 Hz
  the tone moves                it was a beat against the 120 Hz sampling

The loop rate is untouched by that change, so a genuine 3.2 Hz signal keeps
its position in cycles per sample while a beat against the sensor's rate
cannot.

THE SECOND MISSING NUMBER IS FREE. The loop has never been timed. Timing it
once converts every frequency here from cycles per sample into hertz and
retires the assumption the whole analysis rests on.

WHY NONE OF THIS IS FIXED BY A FASTER LOOP. There are two sampling stages:
the sensor decimating to its output rate, which has a filter in front of
it, and the loop resampling those registers at an unlocked rate, which has
no filter at all and cannot have one. The second stage is where the folding
and the beat come from, and it exists because the reading is done by a
program asking rather than by the part delivering. The FIFO removes that
stage: samples are taken on the sensor's own clock, uniformly, and read out
in bursts afterwards. It is reachable over i2c-dev without any kernel
driver, and it is the only route to a spectrum that means anything in
hertz.

THE LOOP RATE, MEASURED AT LAST. Timing the capture itself rather than a
stripped-down imitation of it:

  4000 samples in 31.653 s = 126.4 reads per second
  4000 samples in 31.501 s = 127.0 reads per second

Half a percent apart, so the captures are comparable and the loop is far
steadier than the 3 percent spread of a 200-sample timing suggested.

A FIRST TIMING WAS WRONG, AND WRONG IN A WAY WORTH NAMING. Timing this

  i2ctransfer ... >/dev/null

gave 185 reads a second, and the loop that writes the file is this

  r=$(i2ctransfer ...) ; echo "$i $r"

which forks a subshell, pipes the output back and reads it on every
sample. 185 is the speed of a loop that throws the data away. Timing an
imitation of the thing measures the imitation. The captures run at 126.7.

That also settles a contradiction: the very first burst capture, 1905
unique out of 2905, implied 183 reads a second, and it used a tight loop
with no command substitution. Both numbers are right and they describe
different loops.

SO THE FREQUENCY AXIS IS NOW IN HERTZ. Multiply cycles per sample by
126.7. The peak at 0.0270 is 3.42 Hz, and the axis runs to 63.35 Hz.

THE NOISE FLOOR SCALES AS THE SQUARE ROOT OF BANDWIDTH, TO ONE PERCENT.
Doubling the output rate from 120 Hz to 240 Hz doubles the measurement
bandwidth, so white noise should rise by the square root of two, 1.414.

The duplicate fraction is 0.0 percent at 240 Hz, exactly as it must be when

A CLAIM MADE EARLIER TONIGHT IS WITHDRAWN. The record above says the
shaver's broadband rise of about ten times in the top half of the band is
folded content, and states it as measured rather than suspected. It is not

126.7 can represent 63.35 Hz, so the second sampling stage adds almost no
folding at that setting. The rise is equally consistent with the shaver
genuinely exciting the board across the whole band, which a mechanism
rattling against a circuit board does. Aliasing in the part's own
decimation remains possible. Neither is shown.

THE BEAT TEST, ARITHMETIC DONE BEFORE THE DATA ARRIVES. The loop rate is
unchanged at 126.7, so:

  the tone stays at 0.0270 cyc/sample, 3.42 Hz
      it is a real mechanical vibration and the beat idea is wrong

  the tone moves anywhere else

WHAT THE RUN DOES ESTABLISH. Raising the output rate made the measurement

RULE: for a polled capture, set the output rate at or just below the poll
rate. The part's filter is the only protection the second sampling stage
can have.

THE NEXT TEST IS PRESENCE AND ABSENCE, NOT POSITION. Every available output
rate is 120 Hz times a power of two, so 120 is a harmonic of all of them
and a line near 117 or 123 Hz beats to the same 3.4 Hz at every setting.
Lowering the rate to 30 Hz puts the part's filter near 15 Hz: a real 3.42 Hz
vibration passes and a 117 Hz line is rejected before it can alias.

  tone survives at 3.42 Hz    real vibration
  tone disappears             a beat from a line near 120 Hz

And hold the shaver the same way for both captures this time, full run,
same pressure, or the result is void again.

WHAT THE TONE PROBABLY IS, AND IT IS NOT THE SHAVER'S MECHANISM. Between

modulates the contact force at a few hertz. The strongest line in this
spectrum is most likely the operator.

That is testable and the test is free: tape or clamp the shaver to the
board so no hand touches it, and capture again. If the tone weakens sharply
or moves, it was the grip. If it holds at the same frequency and relative
strength, it belongs to the machine.

A CORRECTION TO THE ADVICE TO RAISE THE OUTPUT RATE. 208 and 416 Hz are not
rates this part offers; the ladder here is 120, 240, 480, 960. More
importantly, raising it is the wrong direction. At 120 Hz output the part
band-limits to 60 Hz and the loop can represent 63.35, so the whole 12 to
25 Hz band is resolved with no folding, and the 120 Hz record does show it,
0.299 mg at 18.71 Hz. At 240 Hz output the part passes to 120 Hz and
everything above 63.35 folds in. 120 Hz output is the correct setting for a
126 Hz loop. That 30 Hz is too low to see the 12 to 25 Hz band is the one
part of the earlier reading that was right.

THREE REFINEMENTS TO THE ABOVE.

ONE, THE LINE IS NOT AT 0.0270 AND SHOULD NOT BE FORCED THERE. It is a hump
from about 0.021 to 0.026 cycles per sample, 2.7 to 3.3 Hz, on Z in both
shaver files and absent from both quiet ones. The 0.0270 of the 240 Hz work
is a neighbouring bin from a different session with a different grip, not
the same line measured again. Carrying a number forward across sessions
because it is nearly the same is how a hump becomes a spurious constant.

TWO, THE AMPLITUDE DROP AT 30 Hz IS THE EXCITATION, NOT THE FILTERING. It
is tempting to attribute 3.85 mg falling to 0.94 mg to the part's
anti-alias filter and the four-fold sample repetition. Neither can do it.
The filter sits near 15 Hz and barely touches 3 Hz. The zero-order hold
from reading each sample about four times is a hold of 31.6 ms, which at
2.98 Hz attenuates by about 1.4 percent.

What did change is the excitation: Z AC rms 6.56 against 1.79 mg, a factor
of 3.7, against a peak ratio of 4.1. And the peak over the record's own AC
rms is 0.59 against 0.53. The hand pressed harder in the first run. The
mechanism matters because attributing it to the filter would imply the
30 Hz setting suppresses a 3 Hz signal, and it does not.

THREE, THE 57 Hz CONTENT IN THE 120 Hz FILE IS PROBABLY NOT AN ALIAS. At
120 Hz output the part passes to 60 Hz and the loop at 126.1 represents to
63.05, so the sensor's whole output band fits inside the loop's band and
nothing folds. Content at 57 Hz is most likely content at 57 Hz. A
structural mode of the shield on its header pins is the natural candidate,
and a tap test settles it: strike the board once, capture, and read the
ring-down frequency, which is a property of the assembly and needs no
shaver at all.

THE DUPLICATE FRACTION IS AN OUTPUT-RATE METER, AND IT SETTLES THE REGISTER
MAP WITHOUT A DATA SHEET. When the loop outruns the part, the fraction of
reads that return an unchanged sample gives the part's rate directly:

  output rate = poll rate x (1 - duplicate fraction)

  CTRL1   poll      duplicates   implied rate   low-nibble ladder
  0x04    126.7 Hz     76.1 %       30.3 Hz          30 Hz
  0x06    126.1 Hz      3.8 %      121.3 Hz         120 Hz

Both within one percent. So on this part the output rate is in CTRL1 bits
3 to 0, with the ladder 1.875, 7.5, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240, 480, 960 Hz, and
NOT in bits 7 to 4 as on the older LSM6 parts where 0x20 is 26 Hz and 0x40
is 104 Hz.

The older map is not merely a worse fit, it is refuted: under it, 0x04 and
0x06 both have 0000 in the upper nibble, which is power-down, and the part
would return nothing at all. It returned 4000 samples of gravity both
times.

This is worth keeping as a technique rather than as a fact about one part.
A loop that is faster than the sensor measures the sensor's rate for free,
needs no documentation, and checks a register write against what the
silicon actually did rather than against what the table said it would do.

THE 12 TO 25 Hz BAND IS NOT GONE FROM THE SLOWER CAPTURES. As a share of
each record's own Z energy:

  CTRL1   Z AC rms    2 to 4 Hz share   12 to 25 Hz share
  0x04      1.79 mg       dominant           24 %
  0x06      6.56 mg       dominant           21 %
  0x07     58.46 mg          minor           59 %

It fell in absolute terms with everything else, because the excitation was
between three and nine times weaker. Its share of the record barely moved.

AND THE BALANCE BETWEEN THE TWO BANDS FLIPS, which is the finding. The
ratio of low-band to mid-band energy is about 3.6 and 4.3 in the two light
captures and about 0.31 in the firm one, an order of magnitude. Two bands
that move in opposite directions with contact pressure are two sources, not
one spectrum scaled up and down:

  2 to 4 Hz     grows relatively with light contact    the hand
  12 to 25 Hz   grows relatively with firm contact     the shaver

That is a better argument for the operator reading than the wandering
frequency was, because it does not depend on judging what frequency a
mechanism ought to have. It only requires that two things respond
differently to how hard the thing is pressed, which is observed.

The percentages above are rough: a band sum under a Hann window is not
exactly Parseval and one of them exceeds 100. The comparison between
records at the same band is sound; the absolute shares are not.

AND ONCE MORE, THE SMALLER PEAK AT 30 Hz IS NOT THE FILTER. The low-pass at
that setting sits near 15 Hz and the tone is at 3 Hz. The peak over the
record's own AC rms is 0.59 at 120 Hz output and 0.53 at 30 Hz, so the
shape is preserved and the level is not. It is the grip.

THE TAPED PAIR, Thursday 1 October 2026. Shaver taped to the board, no hand
touching it, at both output rates.

  mount        dup%    Z rms     strongest Z      X mean    magnitude
  hand 0x04    76.1%   1.79 mg   2.98 Hz  0.94    -5.3 mg   1.0068 g
  hand 0x06     3.8%   6.56 mg   2.71 Hz  3.85    -5.2 mg   1.0068 g

THE OPERATOR READING IS REFUTED. Taping did not remove the low-frequency
tone. It made it nearly four times larger and moved it from 2.71 to
4.87 Hz. A hand was damping the assembly, not driving it.

The general shape of the argument survives and the attribution does not.
The line still belongs to the part of the apparatus that is not bolted
down, but that part is the whole board-and-shaver assembly on whatever it
rests on, and not the hand. Taping stiffened the coupling, which raises a
resonance and removes damping, so the frequency went up and the amplitude
with it. That is the ordinary behaviour of a mass on a spring and it is
what the measurement shows.

A SECOND CLAIM IS WITHDRAWN, AND IT IS THE ONE I WAS PLEASED WITH. The
record above argues that 2 to 4 Hz and 12 to 25 Hz are two sources because
their balance flips with contact pressure, light contact favouring the low
band and firm contact the mid. That rested on comparing the 0x07 record
against the 0x04 and 0x06 ones, across a change of output rate which is
already documented here as folding content from 63 to 120 Hz down into the
band. The mid-band dominance of that record may be folded content rather
than mechanical content, and the comparison cannot tell.

Within matched output rates, where the comparison is sound, the balance
does not flip at all. It moves the same way both times:

  mount       2 to 6 Hz   12 to 25 Hz   ratio
  hand 0x04     1.893        0.434       4.36
  taped 0x04    6.607        1.149       5.75
  hand 0x06     4.867        1.359       3.58
  taped 0x06   24.591        3.941       6.24

Rigid coupling raises the low band relatively, at both rates. There is no
evidence here for two sources with opposing pressure dependence, and the
sentence claiming it was built on exactly the cross-rate comparison this
file spends several pages warning against.

A THIRD VARIABLE MOVED, AND IT SHOULD BE NOTED RATHER THAN IGNORED. The X
bias went from -5.3 to -34.1 mg, a tilt of about 1.6 degrees. Taping the
shaver down changed the assembly's attitude as well as its stiffness. It
does not affect the AC analysis, and it means these captures are not a
single-variable comparison either.

AND THE READBACK FAILED WITHOUT ANYONE NOTICING. After the write of 0x04,
"i2cget -y 1 0x6b 0x10" returned an empty line rather than a value. The
verification step produced nothing and the capture went ahead anyway. What
confirmed the write was the duplicate fraction: 76.1 percent, identical to
every other 0x04 run, which puts the rate at 30.3 Hz.

So the output-rate meter stood in for a verification that silently did not
happen. That is a better argument for it than the convenience of not
needing a data sheet: it measures the part's behaviour, where a readback
only reports a register, and it still works when the readback does not.

THE TAP TEST, Thursday 1 October 2026. Shaver removed, 0x06, four taps at
0.92, 2.46, 3.97 and 5.46 s. Poll 126.85 Hz, 4.9 percent duplicates.

QUIET FLOOR BETWEEN TAPS: 0.42 / 0.37 / 0.58 mg, matching every other still
capture, and NO 2.7 to 4.9 Hz hump. That line needs the shaver running.

CLIPPING. Raw Z reaches 32764 against a rail of 32767, with four samples
within two percent of it. So the tap peak is not measured, only bounded
below at about two g, and the shape of those four samples is not data.

A RING-DOWN SEARCH, AND THE FIRST VERSION OF IT WAS WRONG. Taking 256
samples after each tap and looking for 3 to 6 Hz gave 1.21, 1.53, 26.83 and
0.08 mg. The 26.83 is an artefact of the window: the taps are 1.5 s apart
and a 2.0 s window contains the NEXT tap. Only the fourth window, after the
last tap, contains one impulse and nothing else.

  tap 4, clean window      0.08 mg in 3 to 6 Hz
  a quiet stretch          0.06 mg, the floor
  taped shaver running    28.28 mg at 4.96 Hz

SO A TAP DOES NOT RING THE ASSEMBLY AT 3 TO 6 Hz. At 126.85 Hz a 5 Hz
oscillation is 25 samples per cycle, so unlike the 57 Hz question this
absence is a real measurement and not a sampling limit. An impulse reaching
two g produces nothing above the noise in a band where the running shaver
produces 28 mg.

THAT IS A CONSTRAINT, NOT A CONCLUSION. It sits awkwardly beside the taped
result, and both are sound:

  the frequency moves with the mounting, 2.71 Hz held to 4.87 Hz taped
      which is what a resonance does

  an impulse does not excite it
      which is what a resonance does not do

A lightly damped mode would do both. Something that needs continuous
driving does the first and not the second. A self-excited contact
oscillation, where the shaver rattles or sticks and slips against the board
and the rate depends on contact stiffness, would fit both, and so would a
heavily damped mode that an impulse cannot sustain. Nothing here chooses
between them and the record should not pretend otherwise.

FULL SCALE FOR THE NEXT TAP CAPTURE, AND WHAT IT COSTS. Moving from two g
to eight g makes one count 0.244 mg instead of 0.061. The measured noise
floor is 0.36 mg, which is 5.9 counts at two g and 1.5 at eight, and
quantisation noise of one count spread over root twelve is 0.07 mg. Added
in quadrature that is 0.367 against 0.360, about two percent.

So four times the headroom for two percent of the noise floor, which is a
good trade for an impulse measurement and a bad one for a noise floor.

Verify the change by the raw counts rather than the scaled value: gravity
should fall from about 16500 counts to about 4125, a factor of four. A
decoder still dividing by 16384 would otherwise report a quarter g and
look like a wiring fault.

EIGHT G FULL SCALE CONFIRMED BY WHAT THE PART DID, Thursday 1 October 2026.

  # i2cset -y 1 0x6b 0x17 0x02
  # i2ctransfer -y 1 w1@0x6b 0x2c r2
  0x1c 0x10

0x101c is 4124 counts. At 4096 LSB per g that is 1.00684 g.

Three things at once. The full-scale field is in CTRL8 at 0x17 with 0x02
selecting eight g, which was written here from memory and is now measured
rather than recalled. The scale is 4096 LSB per g. And the check needed no
data sheet: the prediction was a count, the part returned that count, and a
wrong register or a wrong bit position would have left gravity at about
16500.

AND THE CALIBRATION ERROR IS COMMON TO BOTH RANGES, which is new. Gravity
reads 1.00680 g at two g full scale and 1.00684 g at eight g, a difference
of 36 parts per million across a fourfold change of range.

So the 0.68 percent by which this part reads high is not a per-range gain
error. It is one sensitivity error in the part, and a single correction
factor of 0.99324 nulls it at every range rather than needing a constant
per setting. That is worth knowing before anyone writes a calibration
table with four rows in it.

It also rules out the local value of gravity as the explanation, which
would have been worth about 40 parts per million here and not 6800, and
rules out tilt, which lowers a Z reading rather than raising it.

AND THE DECODER MUST CHANGE WITH THE REGISTER. Anything still dividing by
16384 will report a quarter of a g from now on and read exactly like a
wiring fault. The scale and the full-scale write belong in the same edit.
