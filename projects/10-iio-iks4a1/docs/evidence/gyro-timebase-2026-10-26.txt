The driver's timestamps are not real time, by 15.7 per cent
===========================================================

Monday 26 October 2026, Raspberry Pi 3 Model B Plus, bench-iio-image from
commit 8ae1ada, kernel 6.6.63-v8, LSM6DSV16X at 0x6b read through
st_lsm6dsx. Four buffered captures.

WHAT THIS SETTLES. The timestamps IIO delivers for this part are longer
than real time by a constant factor near 1.156, independent of the output
data rate. It is a scale error and not a drift. Any measurement that
integrates against them, which is what the gyroscope method does, is
wrong by that factor and cannot separate it from the quantity being
measured.

WHAT IT DOES NOT SETTLE. The mechanism. The arithmetic below is
consistent with one wrong constant in the driver, and neither the driver
source nor the part's datasheet has been read here. That is an inference
and is marked as one throughout.


1. THE PROOF, WHICH NEEDS NO ARITHMETIC
---------------------------------------

A capture of 28800 scans was bracketed by the board's own clock:

    # echo 1 > buffer/enable && date +%s && dd if=/dev/iio:device4 \
          of=g2.bin bs=1024 count=450 && date +%s
    1793045554
    450+0 records in
    450+0 records out
    1793045616

Its first and last timestamps:

    first    1793045554.903     date before dd  1793045554    +0.903
    last     1793045626.094     date after  dd  1793045616   +10.094

THE LAST SAMPLE IS STAMPED TEN SECONDS AFTER THE READ THAT RETURNED IT
FINISHED. No clock can do that. Nothing else in this file is needed to
know the timestamps are wrong; the rest is needed only to say by how
much and in what shape.


2. THE SHAPE: A CONSTANT FACTOR, NOT A DRIFT
--------------------------------------------

Four captures, two output data rates. Wall clock from /proc/uptime at
10 ms resolution except g2, which used `date` at 1 s and is the weaker
measurement because of it.

    file      nominal   samples   wall clock   ts interval   ts rate
    g2.csv    480 Hz     28800    62.00 s      0.002472 s    404.543 Hz
    g3.csv    240 Hz     14400    61.63 s      0.004944 s    202.262 Hz
    gr2.csv   480 Hz     28800    61.52 s      0.002472 s    404.543 Hz

The interval at 240 Hz is 0.004944 s against 0.002472 at 480, EXACTLY
double. So the error is proportional to the sample interval and carries
no dependence on the rate itself.

Taken against the nominal interval rather than against real time, the
factor is the same number twice over:

    480 Hz:  0.0024719 / (1/480)  =  1.186521
    240 Hz:  0.0049438 / (1/240)  =  1.186558

Five significant figures apart. **The driver assigns each sample an
interval 1.18654 times the nominal one.**

The stretch against REAL time is that factor divided by the part's own
oscillator error, which is why it is not quite identical between runs:

    file      driver/nominal   true/nominal   stretch vs real time
    g3.csv    1.186558         1.027250       1.15508
    gr2.csv   1.186521         1.025400       1.15713

So the part runs 2.5 to 2.7 per cent slow of nominal, which is ordinary
MEMS oscillator tolerance, and the driver is wrong by 18.65 per cent.
Those two compose to the 15.5 to 15.7 per cent seen against the clock.


3. WHAT IT IS CONSISTENT WITH, WHICH IS NOT THE SAME AS WHAT IT IS
------------------------------------------------------------------

A FIFO timestamp counter scaled by the wrong constant produces exactly
this shape: proportional to the interval, independent of the rate, and
unaffected by oscillator error, since a slow oscillator lengthens the
tick and the sample interval together and the ticks-per-sample ratio is
unchanged.

On that reading the assumed tick is too long by 1.18654, so against an
assumed 25 us the actual would be 25 / 1.18654 = 21.07 us.

A FIRST GUESS AT THIS WAS WRONG AND IS RECORDED BECAUSE IT WAS WRONG IN
A USEFUL WAY. The guess was 25 us assumed against 21.75 us actual, a
ratio of 1.14943, and it was made from the 1.1482 that the one-second
`date` resolution allowed. The /proc/uptime measurement is 1.15508, which
is 0.49 per cent away and twelve times its own error bar. The shape of
the explanation survived the better measurement and the specific constant
did not. 21.07 us is what the data says; whether the part specifies that
or something else has not been checked here.


4. WHY IT MATTERS MORE THAN ITS SIZE
------------------------------------

The gyroscope method integrates rate against time. A constant error in
dt is the same constant error in every angle, so a 15.7 per cent error in
the time base is a 15.7 per cent error in the scale factor, against a
reference accuracy of 0.035 per cent. That is four hundred times.

Worse, it is not separable. The product of the gyroscope's gain and the
timestamp scale is what the integral returns, and nothing inside the
capture distinguishes them. A measurement taken this way would have
reported a part 15.7 per cent out of specification, with every internal
consistency check passing.

THE FIX. The time base is the duration of the read, measured on the board
around it, and the timestamps are used only for ordering and for finding
gaps. /proc/uptime at 10 ms over a 60 second capture is 0.017 per cent,
which is below the reference's own accuracy. `date` at 1 s is 1.6 per
cent and is not good enough, which is why the first attempt could not
settle the question it was asked.

scripts/gyro-fit.py refuses to integrate without either --duration or an
explicit --trust-timestamps, and tests/gyro-fit-test.sh drives a fixture
carrying a 1.1551 stretch: trusted, it reads 17.24 per cent where the
truth is 1.50, and with the measured duration it reads 1.5000.
