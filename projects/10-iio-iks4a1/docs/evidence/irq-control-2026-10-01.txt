The interrupt line, measured against its own absence
Thursday 1 October 2026, late evening CEST

THE EXPERIMENT THAT SHOULD HAVE BEEN FIRST

Three runs of one command, differing only in whether the wire is plugged in
at the shield end.

  run  green wire at CN9 pin 6   interrupts/s   samples in 10 s   reader cpu
  A    connected                 244.90         4672              0.90 %
  B    connected                 225.60         4672              0.80 %
  C    DISCONNECTED, dangling      0.00            0              0.00 %

    cd /root && iio-rate fifo lsm6dsv16x_accel 480 64 -d 10

In run C the Pi end stayed on header pin 18 and the shield end was unplugged
from CN9 pin 6 and left touching nothing. /proc/interrupts did not advance by
a single count: 1286 1289 1276 1278 before and after.

WHAT THE CONTROL ESTABLISHES

  1. GPIO24 PICKS UP NOTHING ON ITS OWN. An unconnected input on this header,
     beside a 400 kHz I2C bus, produces exactly zero edges in ten seconds.
     Every edge counted in runs A and B arrived down the wire.

  2. THE FIFO PATH IS ENTIRELY INTERRUPT DRIVEN. Zero interrupts gives zero
     samples, not fewer samples. So every sample ever collected through this
     path depended on a real edge, and the sample count can never distinguish
     a healthy line from a noisy one.

  3. THE STORM IS REAL AND IT IS ON THIS PATH. At 480 Hz with watermark 64
     the expected rate is 480/64 = 7.5 interrupts per second. Measured 225.60.
     That is 30.1 times too many, against a floor now known to be zero rather
     than assumed.

CRITERION 4, WHERE IT ACTUALLY STANDS

  reader CPU        0.80 % at 480 Hz, watermark 64      limit 3 %     PASSES
  interrupts        225.60/s                            limit 8/s     FAILS
  samples           4672 of 4800, 97.3 %                              as before

The CPU half has never been in doubt. The interrupt half fails by a factor of
thirty, and for the first time that number sits on a measurement with a
control rather than on a comparison between two runs that both had the fault
in them.

TWO INFERENCES OF MINE THAT THIS KILLS

FIRST: "the interrupt line delivers nothing". Written Thursday 1 October 2026
in the evening after iio-stream timed out on the accelerometer with IRQ 185
reading 0 0 0 0. The zero was real and the inference was wrong. The
accelerometer was in power-down, where it never asserts INT1, because
iio-stream does not set the sampling frequency and nothing else had. A quiet
line and a dead line produce the same count, and nothing in that observation
separated them.

SECOND: "the fault has reversed direction". Built on the first. It claimed
100001 edges in the morning and zero in the evening were the same wire
failing two opposite ways, and concluded that a wire which both rings and
goes open is a connection problem rather than a threshold problem. The
premise was an artefact: the evening zero was a powered-down sensor, not an
open wire. There was no reversal. One continuous fault, measured twice with
the sensor running and once with it asleep.

The conclusion that came out of it, that the wire wants shortening or series
resistance, happens to survive, but it survives on run C rather than on that
reasoning, and an argument that reaches a right answer from a false premise is
worth less than no argument.

THE SEPARATE DEFECT THIS UNCOVERED

iio-stream does not configure the device. It opens a buffer on whatever the
part is already set to, and the LSM6DSV16X powers up in power-down, so on a
freshly loaded driver the buffer never fills and the refill times out:

    root@eplepi:~# ./iio-stream -d lsm6dsv16x_accel -n 10 -b 100
    refill failed: Connection timed out

After iio-rate had left sampling_frequency at 480, the identical command
returned 1001 lines in 2.198 s. The precondition is real, unstated, and it is
what sent criterion 5 to the magnetometer. The substitution was not forced by
the wiring after all.

WHAT IS STILL NOT KNOWN

The mechanism. Thirty extra edges per real assertion is a number, not a cause.
Reflection on an unterminated line, contact bounce at either connector, and
coupling from the adjacent SCL wire all produce it, and this measurement does
not separate them. What it does rule out is pickup by the GPIO pin itself,
which run C puts at zero.

The next measurements, in order of cost:

  - the same run with the wire as short as it will physically go
  - the same run with a few hundred ohms in series at the CN9 pin 6 end
  - if those move the number, the cause is on the wire; if they do not, it is
    at one of the two connectors or in the shield

A scope on CN9 pin 6 would answer it in one capture, and there is no scope on
this bench.
